The sign says **$6.99 each**.

Underneath, in larger print: **$4.99 each when you buy two or more.**

You do the calculation.

Two cost $9.98. One costs $6.99.

You will need another one. You know that.

But spending the extra $2.99 today means something else does not come home.

So you take one.

Bought one at a time at that price, two cost $13.98. The person who could afford to
spend $2.99 more at the shelf paid $4 less in total.

You spent less at the register.

It cost you more.

---

Four dollars is not a collapse.

It is just a little less left for whatever comes next.

---

Not every shortening comes from being charged a different price.

Sometimes it is what disappears because there is less left.

When I was a teenager, and well into my twenties, I could put five dollars in the tank
and drive for the day.

Not to work. Not to an appointment. Not because anything needed picking up.

I could drive because I wanted to drive. Because the music was playing and the road
kept going.

Now that same freedom costs me twenty dollars.

The necessary journeys remained. They had to.

The first journey to disappear was the one that belonged only to me.

Nothing on the receipt says a little freedom disappeared.

It only says fuel.

---

My car insurance costs $107 a month.

Over twelve months, that is $1,284.

Or I can pay about $1,200 for the year.

The coverage is the same. The car is the same. The risk being insured is the same.

The only difference is whether I can produce twelve months of payments on one day.

If I can, I keep about $84.

If I cannot, I pay it.

A total can be affordable across a year and impossible on one day.

Paying the insurance yearly is not a simple choice. It would save me money, but the
saving is behind a twelve-hundred-dollar door.

The discount is not for committing to a year.

It is for already having a year.

Eighty-four dollars will not look like the reason anything collapsed.

It is just a little less left for whatever comes next.

---

Many of us used to use credit and pay it off.

The balance was temporary. It had an ending.

Some of us now calculate how long we can make the minimum payments and hope some event
arrives in time to reset it.

The event does not have a name yet.

It only has to arrive before the room runs out.

Some people do not calculate anymore.

They already know the math. They know the balance will remain in the red. They know the
interest will keep arriving. They know the minimum payment is not leading them back to
zero.

They use the card anyway.

Not because they do not understand what it will cost later.

Because something has to be paid now.

People choose now to try.

Each minimum payment buys another month.

It also leaves less room inside the month that follows.

The card does not call that a burning fuse.

It calls it **available credit**.

That number is no longer how much there is left to spend.

It is how much of the future can still be pulled into the present.

No single payment looks like a collapse.

It is just a little less left for whatever comes next.

---

There is a name for part of this.

Researchers call it the **poverty premium**: extra costs that can appear when the lower
price sits behind cash, bulk buying, favourable payment methods, lower-cost credit, or
the ability to pay ahead.

It does not mean every product always costs more. It means the cheaper route is often
unavailable to the person who needs it most.

The person with a reserve buys two, pays annually, avoids the interest, and keeps more
of the reserve.

The person without one buys a single item, pays monthly, borrows the difference, and
enters the next month with less.

The next time they meet the same price, they are farther apart than before.

The second price does not merely reveal classes.

It can help create them.

---

The second price appears when paying less requires spending more now.

Sometimes the same number does the sorting.

Finland uses fixed penalties for less serious speeding. But when a driver exceeds the
limit by more than 20 kilometres per hour, the offence can be punished through
day-fines whose amount is determined by the driver's income.

The rule is the same.

The number changes, because the same number would not carry the same weight in every
life.

In Ontario, the fine follows how far over the limit the driver was travelling. It does
not follow the driver's ability to absorb it.

Two drivers can commit the same offence and receive the same base fine.

One pays it from what is left over.

The other puts it on the credit card whose remaining months they have already
calculated.

The number is equal.

The punishment is not.

When a rule carries a fixed price, at what income does the punishment become merely a
fee?

---

We have often explained homelessness to ourselves as the result of choices.

Addiction. Crime. A series of bad decisions. Something that explained why it happened
to them and could not happen to us.

That story was never true for everyone.

But it created distance.

The grocery shopper, the monthly insurance customer, and the person carrying a credit
balance are not always the same person.

But sometimes those pressures meet in one life.

Then the job ends.

The income stops.

The rent does not.

There is no remaining bridge, because the bridge was already being used to reach
Friday.

There may have been illness, addiction, mistakes, none of them, or all of them.

None changes the need for somewhere safe to sleep.

In 2022, one in eight Canadian households reported a previous experience of some form
of homelessness. The most common form was not living on the street.

It was hidden homelessness.

CMHC found that among households evicted in 2021, almost one in five experienced hidden
homelessness in that same year.

Homelessness does not always begin on a sidewalk.

Sometimes it begins on someone else's couch, with a promise that it will only be for a
few nights.

Sometimes it is a motel room paid one night at a time. A borrowed spare room. A car
seat lowered after dark and raised again before anyone notices.

A person can still have a phone. A car. A work history. They can still answer messages
and say they are managing.

They have somewhere to sleep.

They do not have a home.

From outside, it may look as though one job loss caused everything.

It did not.

The job loss was where the fire finally reached.

---

The grocery store did not choose who had $9.98 that day.

The insurer did not choose who had $1,200.

The bank did not choose whose income stopped covering the month.

The government did not choose who could absorb the fine.

Each institution chose only the price, the payment structure, the interest rate, or the
consequence.

The rule did the sorting.

Is this slow separation—one discount, one fee, one interest charge at a time?

And when the same decisions keep moving the same families toward the same edge,
generation after generation—

**has nobody chosen at all?**

*The facts and research concepts in this piece are documented and verifiable.*

- **The poverty premium:** Davies, Sara, Andrea Finney, and Yvette Hartfree. *Paying to
  be poor: uncovering the scale and nature of the poverty premium.* University of
  Bristol Personal Finance Research Centre, 2016. The study examines premiums connected
  to household fuel, insurance, telecommunications, food and groceries, access to
  money, and higher-cost credit.
- **Limits of the general price claim:** Kurtzon, Gregory, and Robert McClelland. “Do
  the Poor Pay More Store-by-Store?” US Bureau of Labor Statistics Working Paper 437,
  2010. The study found no overall rich-poor price difference at the stores households
  patronized, underscoring the need to identify particular mechanisms rather than claim
  that every purchase costs low-income households more.
- **Finnish traffic penalties:** Police of Finland, “Automatic traffic surveillance”
  and “Calculator of fines.” Speeding by no more than 20 km/h is generally handled with
  a fixed traffic penalty fee; traffic crimes including speeding by more than 20 km/h
  can be punished with day-fines determined by income.
- **Ontario speeding fines:** Government of Ontario, “Speeding and aggressive driving.”
  Published fines are calculated per kilometre over the speed limit, with the rate
  changing by speed range rather than income.
- **Homelessness in Canada:** Statistics Canada, “Exiting homelessness: Results from
  the 2022 Canadian Housing Survey,” February 12, 2025. In 2022, 12.1% of Canadian
  households reported a lifetime experience of homelessness; hidden homelessness was
  the most commonly reported form.
- **Eviction and hidden homelessness:** Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation,
  “Evictions in Canada: Links between evictions and homelessness,” September 9, 2025.
  Among households evicted in 2021, 19.1% experienced hidden homelessness in the same
  year.
