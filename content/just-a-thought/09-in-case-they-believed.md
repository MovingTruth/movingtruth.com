You've done it.

A cake in front of you, a little fire for every year, everyone singing slightly off-key. You close your eyes. You make a wish. You don't say it out loud, because everyone knows that if you say it out loud it won't come true. Then you take a breath and put out the flames, all at once if you can.

Nobody at the table calls it a spell.

---

So much of a life is made of this. A ring slid onto a finger, with words said exactly right. A knock on wood. Grace over a meal. A candle lit for someone who has died.

None of it does anything a scientist could measure. You do it anyway, because it *means* something, and meaning something on purpose is one of the oldest things human beings know how to do.

An ordinary act, given intent. That's all a ritual is.

---

Four hundred years ago, the church did it out loud.

New church bells were blessed in a ceremony so close to a baptism that people called it one. Some were cast with words in Latin: *Vivos voco. Mortuos plango. Fulgura frango.* I call the living. I mourn the dead. I break the lightning. When a storm came, the bells were rung against it.

Nobody was hanged for ringing a bell.

In 1590, storms battered the fleet carrying King James VI of Scotland home with his new bride. In East Lothian, a healer and midwife named Agnes Sampson was accused of raising that storm against him. She was shaved, bound, and questioned until she confessed. On January 28, 1591, on Castlehill in Edinburgh, she was strangled and then burned.

The bell and the woman were accused of the very same power. One was blessed for it. The other was burned for it.

---

She was one of tens of thousands.

Not the nine million you may have heard. Historians now put the number executed for witchcraft in Europe at around fifty thousand, most of them in a sixty-year stretch after 1570. Enough that it never needed to be anyone in particular. About three in four of the accused were women, very often old, or poor, or widowed, or alone. It often began with a neighbour: a door closed on someone who had asked for help, then a sick cow, a dead child, and a name already close at hand.

Then came the tests. Her thumbs were bound to her toes and she was lowered into the water. If she floated, the water had refused her. King James explained why in his own book: water would not receive anyone who had shaken off baptism. Most were hauled back out on a rope. The test wasn't meant to kill her. It was meant to decide for her.

Searchers stripped her and pushed pins into her skin, hunting for the one spot that wouldn't bleed or feel. In England, where torture was forbidden on paper, the men who called themselves witchfinders simply kept the accused awake for days, walking them up and down until they said what was wanted. The towns paid them for it.

And a confession was never enough. It had to come with names. In Bamberg in 1628, the town's own mayor, Johannes Junius, was tortured until he named others. Before he was burned, with hands so broken it took him days to finish, he smuggled out a letter to his daughter: *Innocent have I come into prison, innocent have I been tortured, innocent must I die.*

---

Now look at what Agnes Sampson was actually killed for.

Nobody proved a storm. Nobody could have. Take the storm away and look at what is left: a woman with no title and no church behind her, the one people sent for when someone was sick or a child was coming. A woman people already believed.

The danger was never that it worked. The danger was that someone might believe it could.

Once you see that shape, it is everywhere.

---

A book. William Tyndale put the New Testament into English so that, as the story goes, a boy driving a plough could know more scripture than a learned man. On October 6, 1536, outside Brussels, he was strangled and burned. The charge was heresy. But an unlicensed English Bible had been forbidden in England for more than a century already. The fear was older than Tyndale: what ploughboys might believe once they could read it for themselves.

A sky. In 1633, Galileo was made to kneel and take back what he had argued, that the Earth moves around the Sun. He spent the rest of his life under house arrest. The Earth moved the whole time. The trial was about who would be believed when they explained the sky.

A drum. In 1739, enslaved Africans near the Stono River in South Carolina rose up, and the drums, by most accounts, carried the call. The next year, the colony made it illegal for enslaved people to gather, to beat drums, or to be taught to write. A drum can carry a message. Writing can carry an idea.

A dance. From 1885 to 1951, Canada's Indian Act made the potlatch a crime, the ceremony in which nations of the Pacific Coast named their children, mourned their dead, and gave away their wealth. After a great potlatch on Village Island in 1921, forty-five people were arrested, for crimes that included dancing and receiving gifts. To stay out of jail, the community had to surrender its masks and regalia. They were shipped to Ottawa, and about half of what arrived ended up in a museum in Toronto.

A gift is not a crime. A dance is not a crime. A people still believing they were a people was the thing the law was written to stop.

---

Then, quietly, the story changed sides.

In 1736, British law stopped treating witchcraft itself as a crime and made a different crime of *pretending* to have the power. Canada kept its own version in the Criminal Code until December 13, 2018. Two days before it was repealed, a woman in Timmins, Ontario, was charged under it.

First she was dangerous because it worked. Then she was a fraud because it couldn't. Either way, someone else decided what her intent was worth.

---

A bell and a midwife. A translator and a ploughboy. A telescope. A drum. A mask.

Not one of them was punished for harm anyone could show. Each was punished for what someone else might come to believe: that a storm could be answered without a bishop, that a page could be read without a priest, that the sky could be explained without permission, that a call could carry across a field, that a people could remain themselves.

---

*I have a ritual of my own.*

When I look at the clock and it's 10:10, or 11:11, or 12:12, or 1:11, or 2:22, I make a wish. And I know why I saw that time.

Lately it happens every day, all day. Not once or twice. Every time I look at the clock, the numbers repeat.

Four hundred years ago, I might have needed to be careful who I said that to.

---

Somewhere tonight, someone will close their eyes over a cake and wish for something they would never say out loud. Someone else will catch 11:11 on a clock and make a wish of their own. Nobody will come for them.

Nobody announced when that changed. It just did.

Not because intent lost its power. Because, for now, nobody in charge is afraid of what you might believe next.

What would you have to start believing before someone decided it was dangerous?
