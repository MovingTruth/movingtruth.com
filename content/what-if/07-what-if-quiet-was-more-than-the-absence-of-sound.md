The room is quiet.

The lights are off. The television is dark. Nobody is speaking.

Inside the walls, the electricity is still alternating.

In Canada, sixty complete cycles every second. Around the wiring, electric and magnetic fields we cannot see. Outside, power lines carry that rhythm across the landscape, through substations, past houses where people have gone to sleep.

Those fields can be measured. Their strength varies with distance, voltage, current, and the arrangement of the wires.

We know they are there.

We are electrical, too.

The heart has electrical activity that can be recorded through electrodes on the skin. Nerves carry signals through movements of charged particles. These processes produce electric and magnetic fields of their own.

You do not consciously send the instruction that makes your heart beat.

You feel the beat.

---

We chose alternating current over direct current for practical reasons.

Both were possibilities. Both were developed. The electrical world we live in grew from a choice between them.

AC had a decisive advantage: transformers let us raise its voltage, send power efficiently over distance, and lower the voltage again where people needed it. With the technology available then, DC could not do that as economically.

It was a choice made for good reasons, with the tools and knowledge of its time.

We built outward from that choice.

Today, high-voltage direct current can carry electricity over great distances, sometimes more efficiently than AC. Costs and engineering tradeoffs remain. Both have their uses. Both create fields.

With steady DC, the fields can remain steady.

With AC, they alternate.

The choice was practical.

The world it shaped became ordinary.

---

Even sunlight can take the longer route.

A solar panel produces DC. A battery stores energy chemically and supplies DC. In a home using an inverter, that becomes AC for the outlet. The computer plugged into the outlet converts it back to DC.

A journey shaped by the systems we built.

And around those systems, an invisible environment.

We wrapped the Earth in them.

Wires above the ground, power cables beneath the sea. Across the spaces between, we send radio waves and microwaves carrying voices, pictures, instructions—messages travelling farther than their senders will ever go.

Different technologies. Different frequencies.

We built a world that speaks across oceans, through walls, beyond the atmosphere.

Could we have disturbed something along the way?

Something we had not yet learned to listen for?

---

We once thought asbestos was extraordinary.

Strong. Durable. Resistant to fire.

We put it into buildings to protect people. Its usefulness was real. So was the harm its airborne fibres could cause.

That history does not tell us that electrical fields are another asbestos. It reminds us that knowing what something can do for us is not the same as knowing everything it can do.

A reason to keep asking.

And to be willing to accept an answer that changes our minds.

---

The Earth already had a magnetic field before we hung the first wire.

Set a compass on a table. Something you cannot see gives the needle a direction.

Invisible never meant absent.

But invisible does not mean that every explanation we imagine is true, either.

Our bodies’ electrical activity does not establish that intuition is a magnetic sense. It does not prove that the feeling of connection between two people is a field passing between them.

There is no established evidence that ordinary household AC dampens presence, intuition, or some deeper human sensitivity.

That is where the evidence stops.

What follows is a possibility to imagine, not a discovery to announce.

---

What if some of the experiences we give separate names belong to something more connected?

Electrical activity. Awareness. Presence.

The feeling of being fully here.

We know these experiences depend on a living body. We have not established that they are expressions of one underlying field.

But suppose there were a sensitivity we had not yet recognized.

And suppose it became quieter.

Would we notice?

If sight slowly faded, there would still be letters we could no longer read.

If hearing diminished, someone could repeat the sentence.

What would we compare an unnamed sense against?

Especially if we grew up with it already quiet.

We might still work. Speak. Laugh. Fall in love.

We might have no reason to believe anything was missing.

What word would we use for the absence?

Tiredness?

Distance?

Just the way life feels?

---

A compelling possibility can still be wrong.

To investigate this one, we would have to ask more than whether it feels true. What changes? Under which conditions? What could we measure? What result would persuade us to let the idea go?

A power line cannot answer those questions simply by existing.

Neither can a feeling.

But there is already something extraordinary in the room, before we add a single imagined explanation.

A heart keeping time through electrical activity.

A planet giving a compass needle its direction.

Wires carrying an alternating rhythm through the walls.

All present.

Most of it outside our awareness.

When we call the room quiet, how much of it are we actually listening to?
